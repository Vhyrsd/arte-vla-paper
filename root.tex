\begin{abstract}
Vision-language-action (VLA) models typically generate robot actions directly
from visual observations and language instructions, leaving the intended
physical evolution of the scene implicit. We introduce \method{}, a VLA that
predicts how the language-referred target should change before generating an
action. Given multi-view RGB observations and an instruction, \method{} infers an
implicit target representation and autoregressively predicts target effects at
four future horizons. Each effect describes translation, rotation, linear and
angular velocity, and activity. The predicted effects are embedded as tokens and
appended to the visual-language prefix, allowing the action expert to condition
on a compact physical future. Simulator states provide effect supervision during
training but are not required at inference. We evaluate one seed-1000 training
checkpoint per method under three rollout seeds. On all ten LIBERO-Object tasks,
\method{} improves the success rate of a recipe-matched \pihalf{} baseline from
70.9\% to 84.3\%, a 13.4 percentage-point gain over 1,500 paired episodes per
method.
On RoboCasa PickPlace-5, it improves aggregate success from
63.3\% to 67.0\%, with gains on four of five tasks.
Fixed-noise
interventions further show that changing the predicted effect values produces
consistent changes in generated actions. These results suggest that a compact,
target-centric future representation can provide an effective and inspectable
interface between VLA perception and control.
\end{abstract}

\section{INTRODUCTION}
\label{sec:introduction}

Vision-language-action models have emerged as a promising route toward general
robot policies \cite{kawaharazuka2025vision,li2024towards,ma2026survey,shao2025large}. By combining pretrained visual-language representations with
large collections of robot demonstrations, recent VLAs can follow open-vocabulary
instructions and transfer across objects, tasks, and environments
\cite{pi05,din2025vision,kawaharazuka2025vision}. Most existing architectures, however, are optimized to map the
current observation directly to an action sequence \cite{sapkota2025vision, zhong2025survey}. The physical outcome that
connects an instruction to an action---which object should move, in what way,
and over what time scale---remains latent inside the policy.

This direct formulation creates two related difficulties. First, the action
expert must simultaneously resolve language grounding, infer task-relevant
dynamics, and generate precise controls \cite{wang2026world,guo2026action}. Second, the resulting computation is
difficult to inspect: a successful rollout does not reveal whether the policy
anticipated the intended object motion or relied on a less robust correlation \cite{wang2026world, xu2026unmasking}.
Predictive world models and visual chain-of-thought methods address this problem
by forecasting future images or point trajectories before acting
\cite{cotvla,worldvla,atm,track2act}. Although expressive, these intermediate
representations can be high-dimensional and need not isolate the physical state
of the object that is central to the instruction \cite{zhong2026acot}.

We propose \method{}, which inserts a compact target-effect bottleneck into a
pretrained VLA. Rather than predicting a complete future image, the model
compresses its visual-language prefix into an implicit representation of the
primary target and predicts target effects at four horizons.
Each prediction contains a 3-D translation, a 3-D rotation, linear and angular
velocity, and an activity logit. The predictions form an autoregressive chain:
the effect at one horizon is used to predict the next. Horizon-specific hidden
states and effect values are then projected into tokens that condition the
flow-matching action expert.

To physically ground this bottleneck, we construct simulator-derived target
effects as privileged labels. We recover object states from the simulator states
stored in LIBERO~\cite{libero} demonstrations and construct aligned
multi-horizon effect labels. These labels ground the compact bottleneck in
intended object motion at multiple horizons, complementing the end-to-end action
objective.

Our main contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We introduce a compact target-effect bottleneck for
    VLA policies. The bottleneck predicts a four-horizon autoregressive future
    and directly conditions the action expert.
    \item We develop a replay-based labeling pipeline that adds aligned target
    effects to robot demonstrations while preserving their original images,
    actions, states, and language instructions.
    \item We show that \method{} outperforms a matched \pihalf{}~\cite{pi05} baseline on
    LIBERO-Object~\cite{libero} and RoboCasa PickPlace-5~\cite{robocasa2024}. Context-preserving
    interventions further test sensitivity to predicted effect values and their
    temporal order.
\end{itemize}

\section{RELATED WORK}
\label{sec:related_work}

\subsection{Vision-Language-Action Models}

VLA models transfer pretrained vision-language priors to robot control through
diverse manipulation trajectories \cite{openvla}. Octo supports language or goal-image
conditioning and adaptation to new observation and action spaces \cite{octo},
while OpenVLA emphasizes an open, scalable VLA that can be fine-tuned for
downstream manipulation \cite{openvla}. Flow-matching policies avoid
discretizing continuous controls: $\pi_0$ couples a vision-language backbone to
a continuous action expert \cite{pi0}, and \pihalf{} extends this family with
heterogeneous co-training for open-world manipulation \cite{pi05}. These systems
primarily map the current multimodal context directly to actions. \method{} uses
\pihalf{}~\cite{pi05} as its backbone but adds a jointly supervised physical prediction
interface that is consumed by the otherwise unchanged action expert.

\subsection{Predictive Representations for Control}

Predictive representations make intended scene evolution explicit before or
alongside action generation \cite{ding2025understanding}. GR-2 jointly learns video and action prediction
from web videos and robot trajectories \cite{gr2}; CoT-VLA generates future
visual goals before actions \cite{cotvla}; and WorldVLA places image and action
generation in a shared autoregressive formulation \cite{worldvla}. These visual
futures retain appearance and multi-object context, but also encode scene content
irrelevant to the commanded manipulation \cite{xu2026futurevla, finn2017deep}. \method{} neither synthesizes future
observations nor performs explicit planning with learned dynamics. It predicts a
low-dimensional physical change for one language-conditioned target and inserts
it into the action context. Thus, visual prediction preserves rich scene
evolution, whereas target effects expose compact values whose temporal order and
influence on actions can be directly tested.

\subsection{Object and Point Motion as Policy Guidance}

Object-centered conditioning separates task-relevant entities from the scene \cite{kasu2026object,wang2026ocra}.
MOO extracts object cues from the image and command to support manipulation of
unseen categories \cite{moo}, but describes the current target rather than its
desired evolution. Motion representations complement this grounding: Any-point
Trajectory Modeling forecasts arbitrary image-point tracks for policy learning
\cite{atm}, and Track2Act converts predicted goal-conditioned tracks into object
and end-effector motion plans \cite{track2act}. \method{} instead predicts one
instruction-grounded target's multi-horizon 3-D translation, rotation,
velocities, and activity, then injects these effects as tokens into an end-to-end
VLA action expert. Because its target representation is implicit, it is not a
standalone detector or calibrated tracker; the structured effects serve as a
compact supervision and action-conditioning bottleneck.

\section{METHOD}
\label{sec:method}

Figure~\ref{fig:overview} shows the complete computation. \method{} preserves
the original \pihalf{}~\cite{pi05} visual-language prefix and adds a target-effect branch between
prefix encoding and action generation. This branch infers a compact target
latent, predicts four temporally ordered target effects, and projects each
prediction with its horizon-specific hidden state into a token appended to the
original prefix. The action expert therefore retains the original multimodal
context while receiving a structured prediction of the target's future. During
training, simulator replay supervises the effect predictions through an
auxiliary loss; at deployment, every conditioning effect is model-predicted and
no simulator-derived quantity is required.

\subsection{Problem Formulation}

At time $t$, the policy receives $V$ RGB observations
$I_t=(I_t^{(1)},\ldots,I_t^{(V)})$, proprioceptive state $s_t$, and a language
instruction $\ell$. As in \pihalf{}~\cite{pi05}, the normalized state is discretized and
included in the language prompt, while each image is encoded into visual tokens.
Let $X_t=\Phi(I_t,s_t,\ell)$ denote the resulting visual-language
prefix. A conventional VLA directly models an action chunk
$A_t=(a_t,\ldots,a_{t+C-1})$ from $X_t$. We instead introduce an intermediate
target-effect chain $E_t=(e_t^1,\ldots,e_t^H)$ and use the factorization
\begin{align}
p(A_t,E_t\mid I_t,s_t,\ell)
&=\left[\prod_{h=1}^{H}p_{\phi}
  (e_t^h\mid e_t^{<h},X_t)\right] \nonumber\\
&\quad\times p_{\theta}(A_t\mid X_t,T(E_t)),
\label{eq:factorization}
\end{align}
where $T(E_t)$ embeds the predicted effects into the \pihalf{}~\cite{pi05} prefix space. We use
$H=4$ temporal offsets $(k_1,\ldots,k_H)=(1,4,8,16)$, corresponding to
$(0.1,0.4,0.8,1.6)$~s at the 10-Hz control rate. The intermediate chain asks the
model to first represent how the instructed target should evolve and then
generate the complete action chunk conditioned on that evolution. This is a
temporal autoregressive factorization, not a structural causal model.

\subsection{Implicit Target Representation}

The backbone produces $X_t\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}$, including visual patches
from both cameras and tokens from the instruction--state prompt. Using a prefix
validity mask $m_i$, we form a global context $\bar x_t$ and produce one compact
target latent:
\begin{equation}
\bar x_t=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_iX_{t,i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_i},
\qquad z_t=f_{\mathrm{target}}(\bar x_t)\in\mathbb{R}^{20}.
\label{eq:target}
\end{equation}
Here $f_{\mathrm{target}}$ is a layer-normalized two-layer MLP with a 256-D
hidden layer. Our implementation retains the entity-slot dimension of the
shared multi-entity predictor, but $z_t$ occupies its sole valid slot and the
remaining slots are masked. Joint conditioning on vision, language, and state
allows $z_t$ to encode the command-relevant object or articulated part without
an explicit detector or object identifier. Importantly, $z_t$ is not regressed
to the current simulator state. Its semantics arise only through the downstream
future-effect and action objectives, so we call it an \emph{implicit target
representation}, not a calibrated 20-D physical state.

\subsection{Multi-Horizon Effect Chain}

The effect chain first combines the target latent with the pooled backbone
context,
\begin{equation}
g_t=\operatorname{Enc}_{E}
\bigl(f_z(z_t)+f_x(\bar x_t)\bigr).
\end{equation}
All effect hidden states are 256-D; $\operatorname{Enc}_{E}$ and
$\operatorname{Refine}_{E}$ are two-layer, four-head Transformer encoders. The
latter is shared across horizons and applied recursively. With
$\hat e_t^0=\mathbf{0}$ and a learned horizon embedding $u_h$,
\begin{align}
c_t^h&=\operatorname{Refine}_{E}
\bigl(g_t+W_e\hat e_t^{h-1}+u_h\bigr), \\
\hat e_t^h&=f_E(c_t^h).
\label{eq:effect_ar}
\end{align}
Thus, a later prediction receives the model's earlier prediction rather than a
ground-truth previous effect. Each output has the fixed schema
\begin{equation}
\hat e_t^h=[\Delta p_t^h,\Delta r_t^h,v_t^h,\omega_t^h,q_t^h]
\in\mathbb{R}^{13},
\label{eq:effect}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta p_t^h\in\mathbb{R}^{3}$ and
$\Delta r_t^h\in\mathbb{R}^{3}$ describe translation and axis--angle rotation
from $t$ to $t+k_h$, $v_t^h,\omega_t^h\in\mathbb{R}^{3}$ are linear and angular
velocities at $t+k_h$, and $q_t^h$ is an activity logit. Translation and
velocity are expressed in the end-effector frame at $t$. With $R_t$ denoting the
target orientation, the rotation increment is the logarithmic map of
$R_t^{\top}R_{t+k_h}$. This target-centric effect schema separates intended
physical change from the robot controls used to produce it.

\subsection{Conditioning the Action Expert}

The 13-D prediction captures explicit physical quantities, while its hidden
state retains the horizon-specific context used to produce them. We therefore
concatenate each prediction with its hidden state and project the result to the
backbone width:
\begin{equation}
\tau_t^h=f_{\mathrm{token}}([c_t^h;\hat e_t^h])\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.
\label{eq:token}
\end{equation}
In our \pihalf{}~\cite{pi05} backbone, $d=2048$. The four effect tokens are appended to the
original prefix, $\widetilde{X}_t=[X_t;\tau_t^1;\ldots;\tau_t^4]$, and are visible
to the flow-matching action expert. The original prefix is never replaced, so
the expert retains direct access to the images, instruction, and state in
addition to the predicted future. Moreover, supervision-validity masks are used
only in the effect loss: all four predicted tokens remain present during action
training and inference. This prevents episode-boundary information from leaking
into the policy through a label-derived token mask.

Following \pihalf{}~\cite{pi05}, let $\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)$ and $\gamma\in(0,1)$ be the
action-flow noise and sampled flow time. Training constructs
$A_t^{\gamma}=(1-\gamma)A_t+\gamma\epsilon$ with target velocity
$u_t=\epsilon-A_t$. The action expert predicts
$v_{\theta}(A_t^{\gamma},\gamma\mid\widetilde X_t)$ and minimizes
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{action}}=
\mathbb{E}_{A_t,\epsilon,\gamma}
\left[\left\|v_{\theta}(A_t^{\gamma},\gamma\mid\widetilde X_t)-u_t
\right\|_2^2\right].
\label{eq:action_flow}
\end{equation}
At inference, numerical integration starts from Gaussian noise and follows the
predicted vector field from $\gamma=1$ to $0$, yielding the complete action
chunk rather than a residual correction to a separately generated action.

\subsection{Training Objective}

For each valid horizon, simulator replay supplies a continuous target
$y_{t,h}^{1:12}$, activity label $y_{t,h}^{\mathrm{act}}$, and validity mask
$M_{t,h}$. Let $K_t=\max(1,\sum_h M_{t,h})$. The two effect losses are
\begin{align}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{reg}}
&=\mathbb{E}_t\!\left[\frac{1}{K_t}\sum_h M_{t,h}
  \frac{1}{12}\sum_{j=1}^{12}
  \rho\!\left(\hat e_{t,h}^{j}-y_{t,h}^{j}\right)\right], \\
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{act}}
&=\mathbb{E}_t\!\left[\frac{1}{K_t}\sum_h M_{t,h}
  \operatorname{BCEWithLogits}
  (q_t^h,y_{t,h}^{\mathrm{act}})\right],
\label{eq:effect_losses}
\end{align}
where $\rho$ is Smooth-$L_1$. The full end-to-end objective is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{action}}
+\lambda_E\bigl(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{reg}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{act}}\bigr).
\label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}
The main results use $\lambda_E=1$ for LIBERO-Object~\cite{libero} and $\lambda_E=0.1$ for
RoboCasa PickPlace-5~\cite{robocasa2024}; Table~\ref{tab:ablations} reports all three tested weights on both
benchmarks. The reported models apply no component- or class-specific
reweighting: all 12 continuous dimensions are weighted equally, including for
inactive targets, and positive and negative activity labels receive equal weight
before the valid-horizon normalization above. All three loss terms are optimized
jointly. Crucially, there is no teacher forcing in the effect recursion, and the
action expert is always conditioned on predicted effects rather than replay
labels. Training and deployment therefore share the same
target-to-effect-to-action path.

\subsection{Replay-Based Effect Supervision}
\label{sec:effect_supervision}

We obtain structured supervision from simulator states during data enrichment;
at deployment, the model uses only the same images, instruction, and robot state
as the \pihalf{}~\cite{pi05} baseline. For every demonstration frame, the
enrichment procedure restores the stored flattened MuJoCo state and calls the
simulator's kinematic forward pass; it never steps the recorded action. Let
$p_t$, $R_t$, $v_t$, and $\omega_t$ denote the target's position, orientation,
linear velocity, and angular velocity in the restored state, and let
$R_{\mathrm{ee},t}$ denote the current end-effector orientation. Matching stored
states at $t$ and $t+k_h$ yields
\begin{align}
\Delta p_t^h &= R_{\mathrm{ee},t}^{\top}(p_{t+k_h}-p_t), \\
\Delta r_t^h &= \operatorname{Log}(R_t^{\top}R_{t+k_h}), \\
v_t^h &= R_{\mathrm{ee},t}^{\top}v_{t+k_h}, \\
\omega_t^h &= R_{\mathrm{ee},t}^{\top}\omega_{t+k_h}.
\label{eq:effect_labels}
\end{align}
The target is marked active if $\|\Delta p_t^h\|_2$ exceeds $1$~mm,
$\|\Delta r_t^h\|_2$ exceeds $1^{\circ}$, $\|v_t^h\|_2$ exceeds $0.01$~m/s, or
$\|\omega_t^h\|_2$ exceeds $0.05$~rad/s. A horizon is valid only when the same
target exists at both frames and $t+k_h$ remains inside the episode.

Task and episode metadata identify the manipulated object's simulator body;
for RoboCasa PickPlace-5, this is the transported object. We use the stored
demonstration states for LIBERO-Object and the accompanying replay states for
RoboCasa, applying the same horizons, thresholds, and validity rules to both.
Labels remain aligned with the original demonstration frames.

Object identities and task indices select the corresponding supervision labels.
Enrichment preserves the original observations, actions, and instructions.

\subsection{Effect Interventions}

To test what information reaches the action expert, we intervene after effect
prediction while fixing the observation, language, checkpoint, initial action
noise, and flow-integration schedule. \emph{Zero-12D} sets only
the 12 continuous values in Eq.~\eqref{eq:effect} to zero while preserving each
horizon context $c_t^h$ and activity logit. \emph{Reverse} assigns the four
13-D predictions in reverse temporal order, and \emph{Shuffle} substitutes
predictions from another sample. For these three effect-value interventions, the
altered effects are re-encoded with the receiving sample's original hidden
contexts; its visual, language, and current-state information is never swapped.
The resulting tests measure sensitivity to predicted effect values and their
temporal organization while preserving the original multimodal context.

\section{EXPERIMENTS}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

\subsubsection{Benchmark and Data}

We evaluate on LIBERO-Object~\cite{libero}, which contains ten language-conditioned tabletop
manipulation tasks. The training set contains 454 available
demonstration episodes and 66,984 frames. Replay enrichment yields 253,423 valid
target-horizon labels, of which 170,107 are active. We verify frame-level
alignment between the original and enriched datasets and observe a maximum
action-alignment error of $2.90\times10^{-8}$. Terminal horizons without a valid
successor state are masked.

We also evaluate five atomic tasks from RoboCasa~\cite{robocasa2024}, a subset
we denote RoboCasa PickPlace-5: Counter to Cabinet, Counter to Stove, Drawer to
Counter, Sink to Counter, and Toaster to Counter. Both methods use the same
2,517 human demonstrations (760,518 frames) from
\path{lerobot/robocasa_target_human_unified}, with per-episode instructions
from replay metadata. Enrichment yields 2,969,079 valid target-horizon labels,
including 1,404,095 active labels, under the rules in
Section~\ref{sec:effect_supervision}.

\subsubsection{Training Setup}

Both vanilla \pihalf{}~\cite{pi05} and \method{} are initialized from
\texttt{lerobot/pi05\_base} and jointly fine-tune the complete model; we use
neither parameter-efficient adaptation nor a frozen vision encoder. Within each
benchmark, \method{} differs from vanilla \pihalf{}~\cite{pi05} only by the target head, effect
chain, effect-token projection, and the auxiliary loss in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:objective}. The benchmark-specific settings are summarized in
Table~\ref{tab:training_config}.

All runs use AdamW with peak learning rate $2.5\times10^{-5}$, weight decay

at 1.0. After the tabulated linear warmup, the learning rate follows cosine decay

aspect-preserving padding; LIBERO-Object~\cite{libero} uses workspace and wrist
views, whereas RoboCasa PickPlace-5~\cite{robocasa2024} uses two agent views and
one wrist (eye-in-hand) view.

We evaluate the final checkpoint without validation-based model selection or
online environment evaluation during training. Vanilla \pihalf{}~\cite{pi05} has 4.143B
trainable parameters; \method{} has 4.149B, adding 5.381M parameters (0.13\%).
Steady-state checkpoint intervals indicate approximate eight-H20 training times
of 12.5 hours for the LIBERO-Object~\cite{libero} vanilla model and 14.1 hours for \method{}.

\subsubsection{Evaluation Protocol}

For each task, we evaluate both policies from the same 50 demonstration-derived
initial simulator states under rollout seeds 1000, 2000, and 3000. This
produces 500 paired episodes per method and rollout seed, or 1,500 episodes per
method overall. All rollouts use the same seed-1000 training checkpoint for
each method; the three rollout seeds measure stochastic rollout variability,
not variability across independent training runs. Rollouts use synchronous
batches of ten environments, ten flow-matching inference steps, and a fixed time
horizon. Success is recorded
without terminating the episode early. We report per-task and aggregate success,
paired rescue and regression counts, and the exact two-sided McNemar test.
Because these resets
reconstruct expert demonstration states rather than the benchmark's standard
initial-state set, we keep this protocol separate from comparisons to previously
published LIBERO~\cite{libero} results.

For RoboCasa PickPlace-5~\cite{robocasa2024}, each task is evaluated on 20
episodes for each of rollout seeds 1000, 2000, and 3000. This gives 100 episodes per method and
rollout seed, or 300 episodes per method overall. For each rollout seed, the two
methods use identical environment resets and environment-provided instructions.
As for LIBERO, all three rollout seeds use one seed-1000 training checkpoint per
method.

\subsubsection{Baseline and Ablation}

The primary baseline is recipe-matched vanilla \pihalf{}~\cite{pi05}. The only separately
trained ablation varies the auxiliary effect-loss weight over
$\lambda_E\in\{0.1,0.3,1.0\}$ while holding the remaining recipe fixed. We also
apply zero-value, reverse-order, and cross-sample shuffle interventions to the
same \method{} checkpoint, together with an identical-condition repeat control;
these are inference-time sensitivity tests, not separately trained ablations.
The weight sweep tests optimization sensitivity, while the interventions test
whether the trained action expert responds to predicted effect values and their
temporal order. Because we do not
include capacity-matched or structural controls, these experiments do not
isolate the contributions of added parameters, autoregression, or hidden effect
context.

\subsubsection{Metrics}

Closed-loop success is the primary metric. We report per-task and aggregate
success, together with the absolute percentage-point difference from vanilla
\pihalf{}~\cite{pi05}. For the paired LIBERO-Object~\cite{libero} rollouts, we additionally report rescue and
regression counts and an exact two-sided McNemar test. Action sensitivity is
measured by the fraction of action chunks with any numerical change, the maximum
absolute elementwise difference, and the relative root-mean-square (RMS)
distance; the latter two are summarized by their medians across observations.
Trainable-parameter overhead is reported in the training setup.

\subsection{Closed-Loop Performance}

Table~\ref{tab:main_results} compares the benchmark-wise best of the three
evaluated \method{} effect-loss weights with recipe-matched vanilla \pihalf{}~\cite{pi05} on
LIBERO-Object~\cite{libero} and RoboCasa PickPlace-5~\cite{robocasa2024}.
Using one seed-1000 training checkpoint per method and averaging over three
rollout seeds, target-effect conditioning improves mean LIBERO-Object~\cite{libero}
success from 70.9\% to 84.3\%, an absolute gain of 13.4 percentage points.
Pooling the 1,500 paired rollouts,
\method{} produces 380 rescues and 179 regressions; the corresponding exact
two-sided McNemar test yields $p=1.20\times10^{-17}$. On RoboCasa PickPlace-5~\cite{robocasa2024},
aggregate success increases from 63.3\% to 67.0\%, a 3.7-point gain averaged
over the same three rollout seeds.

The aggregate improvement is dominated by two large rescues. On Butter and

uniform per-task benefit nor a benchmark-independent choice of loss weight.

\paragraph{Action sensitivity}

We perform a fixed-noise action audit on 32 observations drawn equally from

\subsection{Discussion and Limitations}

The task-level results show that the aggregate LIBERO-Object~\cite{libero} gain is not a
uniform improvement. The large rescues on Butter and Chocolate Pudding are
consistent with target-effect supervision providing an additional object-motion
signal when the direct policy fails. By contrast, the regressions on Ketchup,
Milk, and BBQ Sauce could reflect inaccurate target effects, entanglement of
object and task context in the implicit target representation, or competition
between effect and visual-language tokens inside the action expert. These
explanations remain hypotheses: distinguishing them requires comparing effect
error and action sensitivity on matched rescue and regression episodes.
On RoboCasa PickPlace-5~\cite{robocasa2024}, four tasks improve and one decreases slightly, but the loss-weight
sweep reverses the LIBERO~\cite{libero} ordering. This indicates that the balance between the
action and auxiliary objectives is benchmark dependent even when the
target-effect representation is unchanged.

The experimental evidence has several further limits. All reported models use
one training seed; Table~\ref{tab:main_results} varies only rollout seeds and
uses benchmark-specific reset protocols. Table~\ref{tab:ablations} likewise
varies rollout seeds rather than independent training initializations.
The current experiments also lack capacity-matched, value-free, and structural
controls. Consequently, they do not isolate the contribution of explicit
physical values from added capacity, autoregressive prediction, or the learned
horizon context carried by each effect token. Likewise, the 32-observation
audit establishes computational dependence rather than an independent
closed-loop benefit of the predicted values.

Finally, effect supervision relies on privileged object states available in
simulation or instrumented datasets. Extending the method to unconstrained
real-world data would require reliable state estimation or pseudo-labeling.
Independent training-seed replication, standard-initialization evaluation,
principled loss-weight selection, matched controls, and closed-loop
interventions over the full task distribution are needed to establish
robustness, causal attribution, and generalization.

\FloatBarrier
\section{CONCLUSION}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented \method{}, which augments a VLA with an autoregressive branch that
predicts structured target effects at four future horizons and conditions the
action expert on their token embeddings.

Using one seed-1000 training checkpoint per method evaluated under three rollout
seeds, the reported \method{} configurations achieve aggregate success rates of
84.3\% versus 70.9\% for the baseline on LIBERO-Object~\cite{libero}, and 67.0\%
versus 63.3\% on RoboCasa PickPlace-5~\cite{robocasa2024}, although the gains vary
substantially across tasks. Fixed-noise interventions show that generated
actions are numerically sensitive to predicted effect values, but do not isolate
an independent closed-loop benefit of those values. Establishing robustness and
attribution will require independent training seeds, matched structural
controls, standard benchmark initializations, and closed-loop interventions
across the full task distribution.
